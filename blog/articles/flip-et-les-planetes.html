Deep sky — exoplanet imaging

FLIP: teaching the telescope's physics to software

5 November 2026 · James Webb Space Telescope, NIRCam instrument · via Ars Technica

The exoplanet catalog keeps growing, and researchers want to move on to the next step: inferring what conditions are like on these worlds, either by imaging them directly or by capturing the light that has passed through their atmosphere. The problem is one of scale: a star is billions of times brighter than the light that reaches us by way of its planet.

The coronagraph's leak

Telescopes can be fitted with coronagraphs, masks that block out most of a star's light. But their effectiveness has limits, caused by noise from subtle variations in the telescope's configuration and pointing between observations. Everything rests on the point spread function (PSF): image a single point of light, and it will not appear as a point. It comes out slightly blurry and spread out, because of imperfections in the hardware and the limits of keeping the telescope pointed over the exposure. The mathematical description of that distortion is the PSF.

Two roads lead to the PSF. You can measure it by observing a known target — effective, but hardware behavior drifts over time and varies with wavelength. Or you can model it from the physics of the instrument — but modern telescopes are so complex that such models carry too many parameters to tune to stay computationally tractable.

A gradient descent on a telescope

This is where the world of AI comes in: we have become quite good at tuning billions of parameters in neural networks, thanks to gradient descent, long optimized on GPUs. A team has developed a process called FLIP (for Physics-based Learning for Imaging Planets — a deliberately loose acronym): a physical model of the NIRCam instrument on the James Webb Space Telescope, with 106 parameters, optimized by gradient descent against data from a real star. On a current GPU, the process takes a little over half an hour. The result is then fine-tuned on the scientific image being analyzed — a critical step, since some variables differ from one observation to the next.

The tests covered two young stars with known exoplanets, HIP 65426 and 51 Eridani, plus a third, TYC 5899, with no known planets but famous for varying a lot between observations. On Webb data, FLIP improved the signal-to-noise ratio by a factor of 1.5 to 2 depending on the case. At the wavelength used to detect carbon dioxide, one of the planets appeared in the Webb data for the first time. On TYC 5899, sensitivity rose by a factor of 15.

See farther, diagnose closer

The payoff goes beyond already-known planets: by lowering the noise, dimmer planets — hence older or lower-mass ones — that had eluded detection should come into view. The researchers also plan to modify FLIP to show where it makes the most dramatic changes to the model: a way of diagnosing what is really going on inside the telescope's hardware. The lesson is lovely: to see farther, software first had to learn how the mirror is made.
