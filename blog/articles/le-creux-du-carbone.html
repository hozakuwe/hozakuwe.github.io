Climate — Global Carbon Project analysis

The carbon dip won't last

10 November 2026 · team of Corinne Le Quéré (University of East Anglia) · via Ars Technica

Every year, global greenhouse-gas emissions rise a little — the series is remarkably regular, far less noisy than temperatures. But 2026 will be the exception: according to an analysis led by Corinne Le Quéré of the University of East Anglia, fossil-fuel emissions are falling by about 0.4 percent from last year. Grudgingly, though: this is probably not the long-awaited peak.

A dip born of a war

Last year, fossil-fuel emissions grew by 1.2 percent — a little above the 2019–2025 average of 0.8 percent a year. This year's ledger reads: a 1.9 percent drop in oil-related emissions, a slight 0.3 percent drop for natural gas, and a 0.6 percent rise for coal. The main cause of the dip is neither a technological virtue nor a collapse in demand: it is the war in Iran, which drove up oil prices — an involuntary conservation effort, so to speak.

Unlike the 2020 pandemic dip, energy demand did not fall this year. It kept growing — but additions of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation covered nearly 70 percent of that growth. Electric vehicles account for only about 20 percent of the fall in oil emissions: the bulk comes from the price.

What clean technology already offsets

Between 2019 and 2025, 59 countries cut their emissions even as their economies grew, while global emissions rose by 0.8 percent a year. The researchers estimate that, without the rollout of electric vehicles and renewables, the rise would have reached 1.9 percent a year. In other words: clean technology does not yet turn the global curve around, but it already halves its slope.

Extending these trends with the IMF's growth projections through 2028, the team expects fossil-fuel emissions to keep rising. Without the oil shock, 2026 would have been a year of increase, like any other.

"If further acceleration of clean technology deployment can be supported through time, a sustained peak and decline in global CO₂ emissions could be a few years away."

The nuance matters: being close to the peak is not being at it. The 2026 dip is a price accident, not a victory. The real news of the year lies elsewhere — the share of energy growth that solar, wind and electric vehicles now absorb without emissions. Only a little more juice is needed to tip the curve.
