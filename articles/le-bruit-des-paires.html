Condensed matter — study published in Science

Listening to electrons pair up in a superconductor

8 November 2026 · Milan Allan (LMU Munich), Ingmar Swart (Utrecht) and their teams · via Ars Technica

Superconductors carry electricity without any resistance, but most only do so within a few degrees of absolute zero. In the late 1980s, copper-based materials — the cuprates — were found to superconduct at temperatures reachable with cheap liquid nitrogen. Nearly forty years later, physicists still do not agree on why they work.

At the heart of the mystery sits a strange state called the pseudogap. Above the temperature at which a cuprate becomes superconducting, some of its electrons seem to vanish: measurements find far fewer of them than expected at the energies where they should be. "Everyone sees it with different probes, but nobody knows why," sums up Milan Allan of Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich.

Two rival explanations

For decades, two families of explanations have faced off. The first holds that the electrons get stuck in some structure — charge density waves, frozen patterns — and that superconductivity develops separately. The second ties the phenomenon directly to superconductivity: the missing electrons would be paired up. These pairs, the Cooper pairs, are what carry current in a superconductor. If they appear before the material turns superconducting, they are a precondition of it — a kind of waiting room where pairs exist but cannot yet move in lockstep.

Settling the question was hard: every instrument sees something, but never anything conclusive. The team chose to listen directly to shot noise — the tiny fluctuations caused by the arrival of individual charges in the current. Allan compares it to rain on a roof: if each drop suddenly becomes twice as heavy, the drumming changes. Likewise, if electrons always arrive two by two, the noise grows.

Four years of electronics

The measurement was made with a scanning tunneling microscope, whose tip passes a current into a cuprate sample (a BSCCO crystal cooled to a few degrees above absolute zero). By adjusting the voltage, the team picked the energy of the electrons they were listening to, a bit like tuning a radio. Making the instrument sensitive enough took years: "there was not a single trick — it was electronics craftsmanship," says Allan.

The result is clean. If the pseudogap came from frozen patterns, the extra noise of pairing should only appear at the energies of superconductivity. Instead, it starts exactly at the pseudogap energy — at every point on the surface, and across samples of different chemical composition. The decisive control plunged the samples into magnetic fields of up to six tesla, which suppress pairing: the noise fell back to the lone-electron level, exactly as expected.

What it changes

In a conventional superconductor, the two ingredients needed — pair formation and phase coherence, the moment electrons begin to move as a single fluid — arrive simultaneously at the critical temperature. In cuprates, the study draws a different sequence: "first you have pre-formed pairs, and only at lower temperatures does phase coherence set in," sums up Ingmar Swart. The energy needed to pull a pair apart, as measured by the team, sits well above that of the superconducting temperature.

"The bottleneck is not getting electrons to pair, but getting the pairs to move together."

For the quest for a room-temperature superconductor, that is a valuable clue: it is not pairing that needs strengthening, but coherence. Forty years after the cuprates were discovered, the pseudogap finally has a sound — and it sounds like drops falling two by two.
